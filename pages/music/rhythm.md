# Rhythm

This page is a working map for studying rhythm from a drumset-centered point of view. The aim is not just “how to count notes,” but how pulses, accents, subdivisions, limbs, sounds, and technique combine into grooves.

Useful references to mine later:

- [The Rhythm and Meter Compendium](https://openbooks.library.baylor.edu/rhythm/) — open rhythm-reading material covering simple meter, compound meter, syncopation, tuplets, and polyrhythms.
- [Advanced Rhythm and Meter](https://odp.library.tamu.edu/stepstomusictheory/chapter/more-rhythm-meter/) — concise theory reference for syncopation, tuplets, and less common meters.
- [Modern Rhythmic Notation](https://iupress.org/9780253338679/modern-rhythmic-notation/) — notation-focused reference for complex rhythm, polymeter, unusual groupings, and twentieth-century rhythmic notation.

## Core time concepts

### Pulse

The recurring sense of time that everything relates to. A drummer may imply it, state it, displace it, or deliberately hide it, but the listener usually needs some pulse to orient around.

### Beat

The counted unit inside the meter. In `4/4`, the four quarter-note beats are the usual counted beats, even if the actual groove emphasizes different places.

### Tempo

The speed of the beat, usually measured in beats per minute. Tempo is not the same thing as density: sixteenth notes at a slow tempo can produce the same stroke rate as eighth notes at a faster tempo.

### Meter

The repeating grouping of beats, such as `4/4`, `3/4`, `6/8`, `7/8`, or `12/8`. Meter defines the expected accent grid.

### Bar

One complete cycle of the meter. Many drum ideas are best understood as one-bar, two-bar, or four-bar phrases.

### Subdivision

The smaller grid inside the beat: eighths, sixteenths, triplets, sextuplets, and so on. Subdivision is the drummer's internal ruler.

### Swing

Swing is an interpretation of subdivision, not a different tempo or meter. In the most common `4/4` usage, the quarter-note beats remain steady while each beat's two eighth notes are unequal. The on-beat eighth is followed by a delayed offbeat `&`, creating a long-short pair. Straight eighths are approximately `1:1`; a textbook triplet model of swing is `2:1`, as though the notes occupy the first and third parts of an eighth-note triplet. Real players use a continuum of ratios, often playing closer to straight at fast tempos and more unevenly at slower tempos.

Swing is therefore not limited to “the quarter-note grid,” but it must be understood relative to a subdivision level. *Eighth-note swing* reshapes the two eighths inside each quarter-note beat. *Sixteenth-note swing* reshapes pairs of sixteenths inside each eighth-note span and is common in some funk, hip-hop, and electronic feels. Compound meters such as `6/8` and `12/8` already divide their beats into threes; they may have a rolling or shuffle-like character without being produced by simply delaying every other written note.

The mathematical delay is only a useful skeleton. A convincing swing feel also involves accent, note length, articulation, dynamics, and interaction. Jazz ride swing, for example, is defined partly by the ride pattern and cymbal sound; it is not merely a quantized stream of late offbeats. The swing ratio may vary slightly across the bar, between musicians, and with tempo. Musicians usually share a recognizable underlying feel without placing every note at exactly the same calculated percentage.

**What swings in an ensemble?** Swing is a common rhythmic reference that the whole band can interpret, but it is not an instruction to shift every sound mechanically. A drummer's ride or hi-hat may state the subdivision most clearly, while kick and snare play fewer notes against it. Notes intended as exact unisons generally remain together, but different voices can sit differently around the shared feel. A bass line can articulate swung eighths while a sustained melody barely exposes the subdivision; a drummer can maintain a swinging ride while placing the snare backbeat slightly behind it. Some arrangements deliberately contrast a straight figure with a swinging rhythm section.

**Swing and shuffle.** The terms overlap. A shuffle normally makes the triplet subdivision explicit and repetitive: a strong long-short pattern, commonly close to the first and third triplet partials, is continuously articulated by a timekeeping voice or riff. Swing is the broader and often more elastic category; it may be subtle, may change with tempo, and need not sound like a pronounced shuffle. A shuffle has swing, broadly speaking, but not every swinging performance is a shuffle.

**Laid back, pushed, and on top.** These describe placement relative to the prevailing pulse or ensemble pocket, rather than the long-short proportion inside each subdivision pair. Playing *laid back* or *behind the beat* means consistently placing a note or voice a little late without losing the tempo. Playing *pushed*, *ahead of the beat*, or *on top of the beat* means placing it a little early, creating forward pressure. These choices can be voice-specific: the hi-hat may define the center of time, the snare may sit behind it, and the kick may sit directly on it or slightly ahead. They can coexist with either straight or swung subdivision.

Deliberate placement remains stable around the reference pulse. *Rushing* and *dragging* usually describe unintended accumulation: the player progressively speeds up or slows down, so the tempo itself moves. A laid-back drummer is not necessarily dragging, and a drummer pushing the groove is not necessarily rushing.

### Tuplet

An even division that does not fit the default subdivision of the meter, such as triplets in simple meter or duplets in compound meter.

### Feel

How the grid is interpreted: straight, swung, shuffled, laid back, pushed, rushed, stiff, loose, heavy, light. Feel is partly timing, partly articulation, and partly sound.

## Accent and placement concepts

### Accent

A note made louder, sharper, higher, longer, or otherwise more important than surrounding notes.

### Backbeat

The common emphasis on beats 2 and 4 in many popular styles, usually played on snare drum.

### Downbeat

The first beat of a bar, or more generally the start of a metrical unit.

### Upbeat

The off-beat before a beat, often counted as “and.” Upbeats can create forward motion.

### Syncopation

Emphasis on a normally weaker part of the grid, or withholding an expected strong beat. Syncopation creates tension against the meter.

### Anticipation

A note placed just before an expected arrival point, making the phrase lean forward.

### Delay

A note placed just after an expected arrival point, making the phrase feel relaxed, heavy, or displaced.

### Displacement

Moving a pattern earlier or later on the grid while preserving its shape.

### Negative space

The rhythm made by what is not played. Rests, gaps, and withheld notes can define the groove as strongly as hits.

## Layering concepts

### Ostinato

A repeated pattern that anchors the groove, often played by hi-hat, ride, or feet.

### Independence

The ability to play different rhythms, dynamics, or articulations with different limbs at the same time.

### Coordination

The practical limb choreography needed to make an independence idea playable.

### Interlocking

Multiple parts fitting together to create one composite rhythm.

### Polyrhythm

Two different equal divisions of the same span, such as 3 against 2 or 5 against 4.

### Polymeter

Two different meter cycles running at the same pulse, such as a three-beat pattern repeating over `4/4`.

### Cross-rhythm

A rhythm that contradicts or cuts across the main meter, often creating temporary ambiguity.

### Euclidean rhythm

A chosen number of hits distributed as evenly as possible around a fixed number of equal slots. Changing the density and rotating the result produces a useful family of ostinatos and interlocking parts. See [Euclidean rhythms](#/music/euclidean-rhythms) for examples—and for why this method does not literally generate every possible rhythm.

## Groove concepts

### Groove

A repeating rhythmic behavior that feels stable enough to support music. A groove combines pattern, sound, dynamics, microtiming, and repetition.

### Beat pattern

The concrete placement of kick, snare, cymbal, and other hits.

### Timekeeping voice

The part that most clearly states the subdivision, usually hi-hat, ride, shaker, or another steady texture.

### Anchor voice

The part that defines the body of the groove, often kick and snare.

### Comping voice

An interactive or improvised layer that comments around the main time, common in jazz ride patterns and more open drumset playing.

### Fill

A phrase that breaks or expands the groove, often leading into a new section.

### Setup

A rhythmic figure that prepares another musician's entrance, accent, or ensemble hit.

### Turnaround

A phrase near the end of a cycle that points back to the beginning.

## Sound and percussion categories

### Pitched percussion

Percussion with clear pitch: timpani, marimba, vibraphone, glockenspiel, steelpan. Some drumset tom writing can behave melodically even though toms are usually not treated as fully pitched.

### Semi-pitched drums

Drums with recognizable high/low shape but not exact harmonic pitch: toms, timbales, rototoms, many hand drums.

### Unpitched attacks

Short, noisy, pattern-oriented sounds: snare cracks, rimshots, kick drum hits, claps, blocks.

### Sustained metal

Longer ringing sounds: crash cymbal, ride cymbal, bell, triangle, gong.

### Closed textures

Short controlled textures: closed hi-hat, muted cymbal, cross-stick, damped drum.

### Open textures

Sounds allowed to ring or bloom: open hi-hat, open crash, floor tom resonance.

### Swept textures

Continuous friction sounds: brushes, swirls, scraped surfaces.

### Shaken textures

Repeated internal impacts: shaker, maraca, tambourine roll, cabasa.

### Scraped textures

Grooved or rough surfaces activated by scraping: guiro, washboard, some brush effects.

### Body and found percussion

Claps, stomps, snaps, table hits, objects, prepared surfaces.

## Drumset orchestration

### Kick

Low-frequency anchor. It can lock with bass, define syncopation, or punctuate phrase shape.

### Snare

Main backbeat and accent voice. It can crack, ghost, buzz, rimshot, cross-stick, or roll.

### Hi-hat

Primary timekeeping texture. Closed, open, foot-chick, splash, edge, and tip sounds all imply different energy.

### Ride

Sustained timekeeping voice, often used for swing, jazz, rock choruses, and more open grooves.

### Toms

Melodic or semi-melodic drums used for fills, tribal patterns, ostinatos, and orchestral contour.

### Cymbals

Crashes, bells, splashes, chinas, stacks, and effects cymbals shape arrivals and color.

### Rim and shell sounds

Cross-stick, rimclick, rimshot, shell hits, and side-stick effects create alternate attack colors.

## Technique concepts

### Grip

How the stick is held. Important variables include fulcrum, pressure, finger involvement, wrist angle, and matched vs traditional grip.

### Rebound

Using the stick's bounce instead of forcing every stroke manually.

### Full stroke

A stroke that starts high and returns high, useful for repeated accents.

### Downstroke

A stroke that starts high and stops low, useful for an accent followed by a soft note.

### Upstroke

A stroke that starts low and ends high, useful for preparing an upcoming accent.

### Tap stroke

A low stroke that stays low, useful for quiet inner notes and ghost notes.

### Double stroke

Two notes from one hand, often using rebound and fingers.

### Triple stroke

Three notes from one hand, often requiring controlled rebound and finger support.

### Buzz stroke

Multiple rapid bounces from one hand, used in buzz rolls and multiple-bounce rolls.

### Moeller motion

A whip-like motion that groups accents and taps efficiently.

### Finger control

Using fingers to control rebound, speed, and repeated strokes.

### Wrist control

Using wrist motion for consistent strokes, accents, and medium-speed playing.

### Arm motion

Using larger motion for power, big accents, or orchestration around the kit.

### Foot technique

Heel-down, heel-up, sliding, swiveling, burying the beater, feathering, and double-stroke foot motions.

## Articulation concepts

### Grace note

A small ornamental note attached to a main note. In drum notation it is normally written smaller, played briefly before the main stroke, and does not occupy its own counted subdivision. Grace notes form flams and drags. They are usually quiet, but their defining feature is their ornamental timing relationship to the main note—not merely their volume.

### Ghost note

A deliberately quiet note that fills out a groove without becoming a main accent. Unlike a grace note, it occupies its own rhythmic position in the measured pattern and can stand alone rather than ornamenting a main stroke. Drum notation commonly puts ghost notes in parentheses.

### Flam

A grace note immediately before a main note, usually played by the opposite hand.

### Drag

Also called a ruff or drag ruff: two quiet grace notes before a main note, commonly played as a same-hand double with the main note on the opposite hand. Some traditions instead reserve “ruff” for alternating grace strokes, so the terminology is not completely uniform.

### Roll

A sustained sound made from repeated strokes, doubles, buzzes, or orchestrated notes.

### Diddle

A doubled hand inside a sticking pattern.

### Rimshot

Stick hits head and rim together for a louder, sharper snare sound.

### Cross-stick

Stick laid across the snare with one end hitting the rim, often used for quieter backbeats.

### Choke

Stopping a ringing cymbal or drum abruptly.

## Practice dimensions

### Counting

Naming the grid out loud or internally.

### Vocalization

Saying the rhythm as syllables before playing it.

### Slow practice

Reducing tempo enough that timing, motion, and sound can be observed.

### Looping

Repeating a short cell until it becomes physically and aurally stable.

### Permutation

Moving the same idea through different starting points, accents, limbs, or subdivisions.

### Orchestration

Moving the same rhythm to different surfaces.

### Dynamics ladder

Practicing the same pattern across levels of loudness and accent contrast.

### Time check

Testing against metronome, gap click, recorded loop, or another musician.
